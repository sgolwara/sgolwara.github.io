# When Does Inspiration Become a Copy?

Brand. What is a brand? Is it the product? The marketing? Maybe the logo? I don’t think it’s any of those. At least, not individually. I think a brand is storytelling. And what is storytelling? Well, everything I just mentioned. And more. And, somehow, maybe none of it. Storytelling is the abstract thing you think of when you remember a brand. It’s the feeling attached to the name. It’s the image that appears in your head when someone mentions it. It’s what makes you recognize something before you’ve even consciously processed the logo.
A brand is a concept.

And “storytelling” is perhaps the closest word we have for encompassing that concept.
Which brings me to something I’ve been thinking about for a while: some of the newer-age brands in India. Now, I should probably make something clear before I start sounding like I’ve declared war on capitalism. I actually *like* a lot of these brands. That’s what makes this complicated.

---

## The First Feeling

I’ve spent a frankly unreasonable amount of time on the internet. So when I started seeing newer Indian brands popping up with incredibly polished online presences, I was happy. Genuinely. For a long time, it felt like Indian brands had a strange problem. We had incredible businesses, incredible products, incredible craftsmanship, and an enormous consumer market, but so much of our branding seemed stuck between two extremes: either aggressively traditional or desperately trying to look “premium.” Then suddenly, there were these new brands. Minimal websites. Beautiful photography. Interesting packaging. Modern typography. Sharp social media. Products that looked like they belonged on the shelves of some impossibly aesthetic store in Copenhagen, Seoul, New York, or London. And I thought: 

*Finally.* 

India is making brands. 

Not just businesses. 

Brands. 

Then something strange happened. I started remembering them. And not in the normal way. I would see a new brand and think, *I’ve seen this before*. But I hadn’t. At least, I didn’t think I had. These companies were relatively young. Some of them were new enough that I had never even heard of them a few months earlier. So how was my brain already treating them as familiar?
 
I went looking. And I fell down the rabbit hole. It took me a while the first time. Eventually, though, I started noticing something I couldn’t unsee. Some of these brands weren’t merely *inspired* by brands that already existed. They looked remarkably, sometimes uncomfortably, similar. The packaging. The colours. The product shapes. The typography. The photography. The tone. The entire *idea* of what the brand was supposed to feel like. And I remember thinking: “*Oh.*” Then: “*They’re copying.*” And then, because apparently my brain enjoys making things unnecessarily complicated:
*“But… why does that bother me so much?”*

---

## I Liked These Brands

This was the part that actually frustrated me. I liked them. I liked their products. I liked their websites. I liked their social media. I liked the identity they had created. And then I found out that the identity I liked wasn’t necessarily something they had created from scratch.

That felt weirdly personal. Which is ridiculous. I had never met these companies. I wasn’t an investor. I wasn’t a designer whose work they had stolen. I wasn’t even particularly invested in the brands themselves. And yet I was angry.

I remember a friend telling me he’d bought a perfume from BLA BLI BLU. 
“BLA BLI BLU,” I said, practically seething. “You know they copied their brand identity, right?” I didn’t know where that anger came from. But it was there. I became that guy.

The moment somebody mentioned one of these brands, I would start ranting about how they had copied someone else. I would explain the similarities. I would point out the references. And eventually I realized:

*Okay.*

*I'm being unreasonable.*

These were still growing companies. They were bringing products and experiences that might previously have been inaccessible or unfamiliar to Indian consumers. They were making things people wanted. They were building businesses.

Wasn’t that enough?

Maybe I was just being pretentious about branding. Maybe this was simply how industries worked. Maybe everybody copied everybody. And then I asked myself one question.

**What is copying?**

And, naturally, back down the rabbit hole I went.

---

# Strategy Can Be Borrowed. Identity Should Be Built.

This is where I think the distinction becomes important.

Because not all copying is the same.

If a company sees another company’s successful pricing model and adapts it, I don’t think that’s a problem. If it studies someone’s distribution strategy? Fine. A direct-to-consumer model? Sure. A subscription system? Alright. A trial period? Why not. A better customer-experience flow? Go ahead. That’s how markets work. Businesses observe one another, learn, adapt, and improve. You don’t need to reinvent the concept of a mattress every time you sell one.

But there is a difference between borrowing a **strategy** and borrowing an **identity**. Marketing strategy is, broadly, how you reach people.

Brand identity is what people come to associate with you. Packaging. Typography. Colour. Product form. Naming. Photography. Tone of voice. Visual language. The personality of the company. And then there is something even more complicated.

Sometimes, strategy itself becomes part of identity.

Take a company like Wakefit and its relationship to the “bed-in-a-box” model popularized by companies such as Casper.

The interesting comparison isn’t necessarily that one mattress looks like another. It’s the *promise*. You don’t need to deal with a showroom. You don’t need to worry about transporting an enormous mattress yourself. You can order it, have it delivered, and have mechanisms that reduce the risk of making such a ridiculous purchase online.

Convenience becomes part of the story. Trust becomes part of the story. The business model becomes part of the brand. And that is why the line isn’t always as clean as:

*"Business model = okay. Packaging = bad."*

It's more complicated than that.

A brand is storytelling.

And sometimes the way a company operates is itself storytelling.

---

# "Inspired By" Is Doing a Lot of Work

This is where the phrase *"inspired by"* starts bothering me. Because inspiration implies transformation. If I see something beautiful and create something of my own from the feeling it gave me, that's inspiration. I saw something. I interpreted it. I changed it. I put myself into it.

Copying is different.

Copying is when you don't just take the lesson. You take the expression of the lesson. And that's the distinction I think gets lost.

Consider Minimalist and The Ordinary. The resemblance people point towards isn't merely one particular colour or one particular font. It's an entire language. Clinical. Clean. Functional. Ingredient-first. No-nonsense. It tells you:

*"We're not here to sell you luxury. We're here to sell you something that works."*

That language is incredibly powerful.

So if another brand adopts not just the philosophy but the same visual and verbal vocabulary, what exactly is it borrowing? A design? Or the *meaning* attached to that design?

Then there are brands like Dot & Key, where people have drawn comparisons to brands such as Glow Recipe and Drunk Elephant.

Again, the question isn't whether two skincare products can both use bright colours.

Of course they can.

The question is what happens when the combination of shape, colour, photography, packaging, tone, and overall presentation becomes familiar enough that you can almost reconstruct the reference in your head. 

At some point, "this belongs to the same category" becomes "this looks like *that*." And that distinction matters. Because packaging isn't decoration. It's the first handshake. Before you try the product, before you read the ingredients, before you develop an opinion about the company, you see the thing. That visual identity is doing work. It's communicating quality. Price. Personality. Trust. Aspirations.

And if you've borrowed that entire system from somebody else, then you've borrowed some of the work they did to create those associations.

---

# The Shortcut Is the Point

This is why successful brands get copied. Not because they look pretty.Because they work.

If another company has already spent years figuring out what colours communicate premium-ness, what packaging communicates trust, what product shape communicates modernity, what photography communicates aspiration, and what tone communicates intelligence, then why spend years figuring it out yourself?

Why take the difficult road when someone else has already paved it?

Look at luggage. A company doesn't merely sell you a box with wheels. It sells you a feeling about travel. This is why comparisons between Mokobara and Away become interesting. The question isn't simply whether two suitcases have similar functions. Of course they do.

The question is whether the new brand is creating its own design language or borrowing the social meaning that another brand already established.

Because people don't buy certain products solely for utility.

They buy them as expressions of taste. And taste is communicated through design. That's the shortcut.

You don't have to convince someone that your product is modern if you've dressed it in a visual language they already associate with modernity.

You don't have to spend years establishing that you're premium if your brand already *looks* like something the consumer has learned to associate with premium products.

You're borrowing the answer to a question you haven't necessarily answered yourself.

---

# But Is It Actually Wrong?

Here's where I had to challenge my own anger.

Because if I'm being honest, copying is everywhere. Music borrows. Fashion borrows. Architecture borrows. Films borrow. Technology is practically built on iteration. Human creativity itself is rarely born in a vacuum.

So where exactly do we draw the line?

I don't think the answer can be:

*"Never resemble anything that came before you."*

That's impossible.

Nor should it be:

*"If you changed one tiny thing, it's original."*

That's equally ridiculous.

Maybe the better question is:

**What did you add?**

Did you take an idea and interpret it through your own culture, your own audience, your own aesthetic, your own philosophy? Did you make something that could stand independently? Or did you identify something that already worked and reproduce enough of its surface-level identity to inherit the benefits?

Because those are two very different creative processes.

And this is where I think consumers matter too.

Consumers aren't stupid. We recognize patterns. We see a certain colour and infer something. We see a particular package and assume a certain quality. We see a particular website and assume a certain level of sophistication. That's precisely why branding works.

Which means that when a company borrows the identity of an established brand, it may also be borrowing the trust and desirability that identity took years to build. Especially when the original brand isn't particularly well known in the local market. The reference becomes invisible. The consumer sees something that feels fresh.

But the company knows where that feeling came from.

---

# The Problem With Everyone Looking "Global"

And this, eventually, became my bigger concern.

Not one brand. Not two brands. The pattern.

Because if every new Indian brand wants to look "global," what happens?

Everyone becomes minimal. Everyone becomes premium. Everyone becomes clean. Everyone uses tasteful colours. Everyone uses carefully curated photography.
 Everyone talks about craftsmanship, quality, community, sustainability, authenticity, and disruption. Everyone starts looking like everyone else. And then we've arrived at a rather funny situation.

Indian companies trying desperately not to look stereotypically Indian begin looking like slightly different versions of the same international template.

We have an extraordinary amount of cultural material to work with.

Thousands of years of visual traditions. Hundreds of languages. Different regional aesthetics. Craft traditions. Architecture. Textiles.Food. Mythology. Modern Indian urban culture.

The strange, chaotic, beautiful collision of old and new that makes India what it is.

And yet sometimes the safest way to make an Indian brand look credible is apparently to make it look like it could have been founded in Brooklyn.

That feels like a missed opportunity.

Not because Indian brands need to be traditionally Indian.

They absolutely don't.

But because *being Indian doesn't have to mean looking traditional*, and looking modern doesn't have to mean looking Western.

There is an enormous creative space between those two things. I'd love to see more brands explore it.

---

# So What Do We Call It?

Maybe that's the question I've been circling this entire time.

What do we call it? Inspiration? Benchmarking? Category awareness? Adaptation? Strategic borrowing? Or just... copying?

I don't think every example belongs in the same category.

Some similarities are genuinely coincidental. Some are simply the result of businesses solving the same problem. Some are reasonable adaptations of successful models. Some are debatable. And some are so obvious that pretending otherwise feels silly.

But I think the conversation is still worth having.

Because the point isn't to shame Indian companies for learning from global brands.

They should learn from them.

Study them.

Dissect them.

Understand why they succeeded.

Take their strategies apart.

Figure out what their customers love.

Then do something better.

Something different.

Something *theirs*.

That's the distinction I'm interested in.

Are you learning **from** another brand?

Or are you using another brand as a template for becoming relevant? Because those aren't the same thing.

---

# Rent an Identity or Build One?

And perhaps this is where I finally understand why I was so irrationally angry about BLA BLI BLU in the first place.

It wasn't really about a perfume. It wasn't even really about copying. It was about something much more fundamental.

I like brands because I like stories.

I like the thought that somewhere, a group of people sat down and asked:

*"Who are we?"*

*"What do we want people to feel when they see us?"*

*"What do we believe?"*

*"What makes us different?"*

And then they spent years answering those questions. That's what makes a brand fascinating to me. Not the logo. Not the colour palette. Not the Instagram account.

The thought behind all of it.

So when another company appears to take those answers without doing the questioning themselves, it feels hollow.

Maybe that's why it bothered me.

Maybe I wasn't angry because someone copied a bottle.

I was angry because I thought someone had skipped the part where they were supposed to figure out who they were. And perhaps that's the real danger of copying. Not that the original brand loses its identity. But that the copy never develops one of its own.

A company can build a business that way. It can make money. It can acquire customers. It can grow.

But eventually, if you strip away everything that was borrowed, you're left with a rather uncomfortable question:

**What was yours?**

A business? Yes. A product? Probably. A strategy? Maybe. But a brand? That's the part I'm still not convinced about.

Because a brand is storytelling.

And you can borrow someone else's plot. You can borrow their aesthetic. You can borrow their vocabulary. You can even borrow their business model. But sooner or later, you have to tell your own story.

Otherwise, you're not really building a brand.

You're renting one.

And I think Indian brands deserve better than that. More importantly, I think Indian consumers deserve to be given something genuinely new. So perhaps the question we should be asking isn't:

*"Does this brand look global?"*

Maybe it's:

**"What is ours?"**
